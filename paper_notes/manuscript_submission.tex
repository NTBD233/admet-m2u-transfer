\begin{abstract}
Low-resource ADMET prediction is a persistent challenge in early-stage drug
discovery: labeled assays are scarce, while inexpensive molecular knowledge
such as physicochemical descriptors is readily available. We study a
training-time descriptor transfer setting in which descriptors may guide model
learning but are not required by the deployed predictor. To this end, we
develop Descriptor-Teacher AdapterFusion, a lightweight ECFP4-only student
that combines a pseudo-descriptor adapter, descriptor reconstruction
supervision, and prediction distillation from an ECFP4+descriptor random
forest teacher. Across five regression ADMET datasets, three low-resource
training ratios, and three random seeds, fixed teacher distillation with
$\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$ improves AdapterFusion in 11/15
dataset-ratio settings, with an average RMSE change of -0.0656 relative to the
undistilled AdapterFusion baseline. Descriptor-access random forests remain
stronger in most settings, so the contribution is controlled descriptor
knowledge transfer into an ECFP-only student rather than an overall ADMET
state-of-the-art claim. A lambda sweep and adaptive-weighting ablation further
show that naive validation-based adaptive distillation is not sufficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Accurate ADMET prediction is important for early drug discovery because
absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity properties affect
which candidate molecules can progress beyond screening
\citep{Vamathevan2019,Butler2018,Xiong2021ADMETlab}. Many ADMET tasks are
low-resource: labels are expensive to obtain, measurements vary across assays,
and each property may have a limited number of reliable annotated compounds.
This motivates models that can use auxiliary molecular knowledge without
complicating the predictor that is ultimately deployed.

Molecular descriptors provide one such source of auxiliary knowledge. RDKit
physicochemical descriptors are inexpensive to compute and often correlate with
ADMET behavior \citep{RDKit2026}. A direct way to exploit them is to
concatenate descriptors with molecular fingerprints or use descriptor-based
traditional models. This is an important descriptor-access setting, but it is
not the only useful formulation. In applications where the deployed model
should remain lightweight, descriptor-free, or compatible with an existing
fingerprint-only pipeline, it is preferable to use descriptors as training-time
knowledge while preserving ECFP-only inference. This creates a multi-to-uni
transfer problem: can descriptor knowledge improve a model that ultimately
uses only ECFP4 fingerprints \citep{Rogers2010}?

This paper studies descriptor-guided multi-to-uni transfer for low-resource
regression ADMET prediction. We begin with an ECFP4 MLP baseline, compare
simple auxiliary descriptor prediction with a structured AdapterFusion student,
and then add teacher prediction distillation from an ECFP4+descriptor random
forest. The central question is deliberately narrow: can descriptor-access
knowledge improve an ECFP-only student under low-resource regression ADMET
splits? The results are positive but bounded. Teacher distillation improves
AdapterFusion in most regression settings, while descriptor-access random
forest controls remain strong.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate low-resource regression ADMET prediction as a controlled
  training-time descriptor transfer problem with ECFP-only inference.
  \item We introduce Descriptor-Teacher AdapterFusion, which augments an
  ECFP-only AdapterFusion student with descriptor reconstruction and
  descriptor-access random forest teacher distillation.
  \item We evaluate descriptor prediction, AdapterFusion, descriptor-access
  controls, tree-based baselines, and teacher-distilled AdapterFusion under
  shared dataset-ratio-seed splits.
  \item We show that fixed descriptor-teacher distillation improves
  AdapterFusion in 11/15 regression dataset-ratio settings and report lambda
  sensitivity, validation-selected lambda analysis, and a negative
  adaptive-weighting ablation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Molecular representation learning has increasingly used auxiliary objectives
to inject chemical knowledge into learned representations. Molecular language
models such as MolBERT motivate domain-relevant pretraining and auxiliary
tasks rather than relying only on generic representation learning objectives
\citep{Fabian2020MolBERT}. Graph pretraining methods such as GNN pretraining,
GROVER, and GraphMVP similarly use self-supervised or cross-view objectives to
improve downstream molecular prediction
\citep{Hu2020PretrainGNN,Rong2020GROVER,Liu2022GraphMVP}. Our study does not
introduce a large pretrained encoder. Instead, it isolates a narrower question:
whether a cheap and interpretable descriptor modality can supervise an
ECFP-only predictor under low-resource ADMET splits.

Auxiliary learning and task-specific adaptation are also relevant. A simple
auxiliary objective may not transfer all relevant information to the downstream
task; the architecture through which auxiliary information is routed can matter
\citep{Ruder2017,Dey2024AuxAdapt}. The closest conceptual motivation is
multi-to-uni molecular representation learning, including M2UMol-style
knowledge transfer \citep{Xiong2026M2UMol}. Our setting is controlled:
descriptors are the auxiliary modality, ECFP4 is the inference modality, and
ADMET prediction is the downstream task. We further add teacher-student
distillation \citep{Hinton2015}, using a descriptor-access molecular property
model as the teacher rather than a larger neural encoder.

ADMET benchmark modeling provides the application context. We use TDC-style
ADMET datasets and evaluate low-resource splits across regression and
classification properties \citep{Huang2021TDC}. MoleculeNet provides an earlier
benchmarking reference for molecular property prediction metrics, splits, and
baselines \citep{Wu2018MoleculeNet}. Fingerprint and descriptor models remain
necessary controls in cheminformatics, while graph neural networks and directed
message-passing models provide stronger learned-representation baselines in
larger-scale studies
\citep{Kearnes2016GraphConv,Yang2019DMPNN,Xiong2020AttentiveFP,Heid2024Chemprop}.
Tree ensembles are included because random forests and gradient boosting
methods remain competitive for tabular molecular features
\citep{Breiman2001,Chen2016XGBoost,Ke2017LightGBM,Tian2022ADMETBoost}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem setting}

Each molecule has an ECFP4 fingerprint $x_{\mathrm{ecfp}}$, an optional
descriptor vector $x_{\mathrm{desc}}$, and a target $y$. The inference
constraint is that the student model uses only $x_{\mathrm{ecfp}}$ at test
time. Descriptor information may be used during training as auxiliary
supervision or through teacher predictions. We focus on regression tasks, where
the task loss is mean squared error. We use ``descriptor-access'' for models
that consume true descriptors at inference time, and ``ECFP-only'' for
students that do not.

\subsection{ECFP encoder and descriptor prediction}

The base ECFP model maps a 2048-dimensional ECFP4 fingerprint to a
128-dimensional latent representation using a two-layer MLP:
\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{fp}} = f_{\mathrm{fp}}(x_{\mathrm{ecfp}}).
\label{eq:ecfp_encoder}
\end{equation}
The simple descriptor-prediction baseline adds an auxiliary head that predicts
standardized RDKit descriptors from $z_{\mathrm{fp}}$. This baseline tests
whether descriptor supervision alone is enough to shape the ECFP
representation. Its training objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}} +
\lambda_{\mathrm{transfer}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{desc}},
\label{eq:descpred_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{transfer}}=0.1$. The descriptor vector contains nine
RDKit descriptors: MolWt, LogP, TPSA, HBA, HBD, rotatable bonds, aromatic ring
count, heavy atom count, and ring count.

\subsection{AdapterFusion student}

The AdapterFusion student uses the same ECFP encoder but introduces a
pseudo-descriptor adapter. Instead of forcing the downstream predictor to use
only the original fingerprint representation, the student learns an
intermediate representation intended to carry descriptor-like information:
\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{desc}} = f_{\mathrm{adapter}}(z_{\mathrm{fp}}).
\label{eq:adapter}
\end{equation}
A learned fusion gate combines the original ECFP representation and the
pseudo-descriptor representation:
\begin{align}
g &= \sigma(f_{\mathrm{gate}}([z_{\mathrm{fp}}, z_{\mathrm{desc}}])),\\
z_{\mathrm{fused}} &= g \odot z_{\mathrm{fp}} + (1-g)\odot z_{\mathrm{desc}}.
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{align}
The target prediction head receives $z_{\mathrm{fused}}$, while the descriptor
prediction head predicts descriptors from $z_{\mathrm{desc}}$. This design
separates the source of descriptor supervision from the inference interface:
the student is trained to form descriptor-informed features, but no true
descriptors are consumed at test time.

\subsection{Descriptor-teacher distillation}

We include descriptor-access controls to estimate the value of direct
descriptor information. The strongest descriptor-access teacher used here is an
ECFP4+descriptor random forest. This teacher is used only during training; the
distilled student keeps the AdapterFusion architecture and adds a teacher
prediction loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teacher}} =
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{y}_{\mathrm{student}}, \hat{y}_{\mathrm{teacher}}).
\label{eq:teacher_loss}
\end{equation}
The final objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} =
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}} +
\lambda_{\mathrm{transfer}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{desc}} +
\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teacher}}.
\label{eq:distill_loss}
\end{equation}
The main configuration uses $\lambda_{\mathrm{transfer}}=0.1$ and
$\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$, selected from the fixed lambda sweep over
$\{0.01,0.1,0.3,1.0\}$. The teacher here is specifically a descriptor-access
molecular property model, so the distillation target encodes descriptor-informed
prediction behavior while preserving ECFP-only inference for the student.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets and protocol}

The main regression benchmark includes five TDC ADMET tasks: Caco-2
permeability, lipophilicity, aqueous solubility, volume of distribution, and
plasma protein binding. Supplementary classification experiments cover five
standard binary ADMET endpoints. The main claims are restricted to regression
tasks because the descriptor-teacher distillation signal is more consistent in
this setting.

Each dataset is evaluated with training ratios of 10\%, 20\%, and 50\%. Each
dataset-ratio setting is run with seeds 42, 123, and 3407. All models use the
same dataset-ratio-seed splits, so comparisons are paired by construction.
Descriptor scalers are fit only on the training split and then applied to
validation and test splits.

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}

We compare ECFP4 MLP, ECFP4 MLP with descriptor prediction, AdapterFusion,
descriptor-concatenation MLP, ECFP4 random forest, descriptor random forest,
ECFP4+descriptor random forest, and Descriptor-Teacher AdapterFusion.
Descriptor-concatenation and descriptor random forest models use descriptors at
inference and are therefore descriptor-access controls rather than ECFP-only
students. They provide a reference for how much predictive information is
available when descriptors are directly accessible.

Regression tasks use MAE and RMSE, with RMSE as the primary metric.
Classification tasks use ROC-AUC and PR-AUC and are reported only as
supplementary reference results. All tables report mean$\pm$standard deviation
over three seeds.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Main regression results}

\input{../paper_tables/table1_regression_main_compact.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:main_regression} reports the main low-resource regression
results.
The descriptor-access random forest is a strong control across the benchmark,
indicating that the selected RDKit descriptors contain useful ADMET-relevant
information. This establishes the motivation for transfer: descriptor
knowledge is predictive, but directly using descriptors changes the inference
setting.

Among ECFP-only neural models, AdapterFusion is stronger than simple
descriptor prediction in all 15 regression dataset-ratio settings. This
suggests that the transfer path matters: predicting descriptors as a side task
is less effective than routing the fingerprint representation through a
pseudo-descriptor adapter and fusing it into the task representation.

Teacher distillation further improves AdapterFusion. With
$\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$, distilled AdapterFusion improves over base
AdapterFusion in 11/15 regression settings, with a mean RMSE delta of -0.0656.
The gains are most visible in settings where the descriptor-access teacher has
a large advantage over the ECFP-only student, such as \texttt{ppbr\_az}. The
distilled student still trails the descriptor-access RF teacher in most
settings. The appropriate interpretation is therefore partial transfer of
descriptor-access knowledge into an ECFP-only student, not a replacement for
descriptor-access baselines.

\subsection{Lambda sensitivity}

\input{../paper_tables/table3_lambda_summary.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:lambda_summary} summarizes the fixed distillation lambda sweep.
The strongest global fixed value is $\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$. Smaller
lambdas can be better for individual dataset-ratio settings, but they do not
improve aggregate performance. Validation-selected lambda is useful as a
diagnostic analysis but beats fixed $\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$ in only
2/15 settings, while still beating base AdapterFusion in 12/15 settings. This
supports using a simple fixed value as the main configuration and reporting
lambda sensitivity as an ablation.

Figure~\ref{fig:teacher_gap} provides a diagnostic view of when distillation
helps. A larger descriptor-teacher advantage can create room for improvement,
but teacher advantage alone does not guarantee student gain. This motivates
the adaptive-weighting analysis below and suggests that future variants should
model teacher reliability more selectively.

\subsection{Adaptive weighting as a negative ablation}

\input{../paper_tables/table4_adaptive_summary_compact.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:adaptive_summary} summarizes the validation teacher-advantage
adaptive weighting rule. The adaptive rule improves over base AdapterFusion in
9/15 settings, but beats fixed $\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$ in only 3/15
settings and beats the best fixed lambda in only 1/15 settings. Its mean RMSE
delta relative to fixed $\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$ is 0.0817, indicating
worse average performance. This negative result is informative: a coarse
validation-level teacher advantage is not a sufficient reliability signal for
adaptive distillation. More selective teacher weighting likely requires
sample-level uncertainty, teacher-student disagreement, or learned reliability
estimation.

\subsection{Classification reference}

Classification results are reported as supplementary reference results. They
are less stable than the regression results, and descriptor-access controls are
often strong. We therefore do not use classification results as the main
evidence for Descriptor-Teacher AdapterFusion.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments support a controlled descriptor-guided transfer story.
Descriptor information is valuable, and descriptor-access baselines remain
strong. The contribution is not to replace descriptor-access random forests,
but to show that descriptor-access knowledge can partially improve an ECFP-only
neural student under low-resource regression ADMET settings.

The results also clarify which parts of the transfer mechanism matter.
AdapterFusion improves over plain descriptor prediction, suggesting that the
architecture used to route auxiliary knowledge is important. Teacher
distillation provides an additional improvement by aligning the ECFP-only
student with predictions from a descriptor-access random forest teacher. The
lambda sweep shows that stronger teacher supervision is useful on aggregate,
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$ as the best fixed global setting.
However, the dataset-specific best lambda varies, and neither
validation-selected lambda nor the tested adaptive rule clearly improves over
the fixed setting. This points to a remaining methodological challenge: useful
descriptor teachers should be trusted selectively, but simple validation-level
rules are not reliable enough.

\section{Limitations}

This work has several limitations. First, the main claim is restricted to
regression ADMET tasks. Classification results are included only as
supplementary evidence because improvements are less consistent. Second, the
student model is lightweight and ECFP-based; the method has not yet been tested
with large pretrained molecular encoders. Third, descriptor-access random
forests remain stronger than the distilled student in most settings, which
limits the claim to transfer under ECFP-only inference rather than superiority
over descriptor-access models. Finally, the adaptive teacher-weighting strategy
tested here is intentionally simple and performs worse than fixed distillation;
more principled sample-level or learned teacher weighting remains future work.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented Descriptor-Teacher AdapterFusion for low-resource regression
ADMET prediction with ECFP-only inference. The method trains an AdapterFusion
student with descriptor reconstruction and teacher prediction distillation from
an ECFP4+descriptor random forest. With
$\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}=1.0$, the distilled student improves AdapterFusion
in 11/15 regression dataset-ratio settings while still trailing
descriptor-access random forests in most cases. These results support
descriptor-teacher distillation as a useful but bounded mechanism for
transferring descriptor knowledge into lightweight fingerprint-only models.
